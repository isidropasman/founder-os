### P1 — Growth is the definition, not a goal among others
Claim: A startup is defined by its growth rate, so growth is the number that decides whether
anything else matters. Optimizing for it forces the subordinate decisions.
Source: paul-graham/growth — quoted: "A startup is a company designed to grow fast."
Applies when: Choosing which metric to steer by; deciding whether a workstream matters
Conflicts with: —

### P2 — Everything else follows from growth
Claim: Age, technology, funding and exits are all incidental. When a founder is weighing
activities, the test is which one changes the growth rate.
Source: paul-graham/growth — quoted: "The only essential thing is growth."
Applies when: Weekly prioritization; deciding what to stop doing
Conflicts with: —

### P3 — Recruiting users by hand is the job, not a detour
Claim: Early on the founder should personally go and get users one at a time. The instinct
to build automation or wait for organic growth is usually avoidance.
Source: paul-graham/ds — quoted: "The most common unscalable thing founders have to do at the start is to recruit users manually."
Applies when: Growth has stalled early; the plan is mostly building and little selling
Conflicts with: —

### P4 — Delight a few users rather than serving many adequately
Claim: Early on, take deliberately unscalable measures to make existing users happy. Breadth
at this stage hides the fact that nobody loves the product.
Source: paul-graham/ds — quoted: "You should take extraordinary measures not just to acquire users, but also to make them happy."
Applies when: Retention is weak; founder is deciding between going wider and going deeper
Conflicts with: —

### P5 — There is essentially one failure mode
Claim: Every other startup mistake is a way of arriving at the same place: building something
nobody wants. Diagnose against that before diagnosing anything else.
Source: paul-graham/startupmistakes — quoted: "In a sense there's just one mistake that kills startups: not making something users want."
Applies when: Diagnosing churn, stalled growth, or a failing launch
Conflicts with: —

### P6 — The test for an idea is want it, build it, others miss it
Claim: Evaluate an idea against three conditions at once rather than on enthusiasm — the
founders want it themselves, can build it themselves, and few others see it as worth doing.
Source: paul-graham/startupideas — quoted: "The very best startup ideas tend to have three things in common: they're something the founders themselves want, that they themselves can build, and that few others realize are worth doing."
Applies when: Evaluating a pivot or a new direction
Conflicts with: —

### P7 — Notice what is missing rather than brainstorm
Claim: Good ideas are noticed from a position of unusual vantage, not generated by trying to
think of businesses.
Source: paul-graham/startupideas — quoted: "Live in the future, then build what's missing."
Applies when: Founder is searching for a direction or considering a pivot
Conflicts with: —

### P8 — The best ideas look bad at first
Claim: An idea being obviously good is weak evidence, because obviously good ideas are
already crowded. Initial implausibility is not a reason to reject a direction.
Source: paul-graham/swan — quoted: "the best startup ideas seem at first like bad ideas"
Applies when: Evaluating a direction others dismiss; deciding whether crowding is fatal
Conflicts with: —

### P9 — Failure is a decision, not an event
Claim: Startups almost never die from a sudden external blow; they die when the founder
gives up. So the question in a bad week is whether anything has actually become impossible.
Source: paul-graham/die — quoted: "Startups rarely die in mid keystroke."
Applies when: Founder is demoralized; deciding whether to persist or stop
Conflicts with: —

### P10 — Release early because it forces contact with reality
Claim: Shipping something minimal early is valuable less for the feedback than for the
pressure it puts on the founder to confront whether the thing works.
Source: paul-graham/startuplessons — quoted: "Perhaps the most important reason to release early, though, is that it makes you work harder."
Applies when: Launch keeps sliding; scope is being added before shipping
Conflicts with: —

### P11 — Founder instincts are unreliable in startups specifically
Claim: The situation is counterintuitive enough that a founder's gut is a poor guide, which
is the argument for an explicit framework over feel.
Source: paul-graham/before — quoted: "starting a startup is a task where you can't always trust your instincts"
Applies when: A decision is being made on gut feel; founder bias is suspected
Conflicts with: —

### P12 — Knowing about startups matters less than knowing your users
Claim: Startup expertise is low-value compared to domain and user knowledge, so time spent
studying startups is usually time not spent with users.
Source: paul-graham/before — quoted: "it's not that important to know a lot about startups"
Applies when: Founder is consuming startup content instead of talking to customers
Conflicts with: —

### F1 — Startups in 13 Sentences
When to use: Auditing a week or a quarter against the short list of things that matter early.
Source: paul-graham/13sentences
Steps:
1. Pick good cofounders.
2. Launch fast.
3. Let your idea evolve.
4. Understand your users.
5. Better to make a few users love you than a lot ambivalent.
6. Offer surprisingly good customer service.
7. You make what you measure.
8. Spend little.
9. Get ramen profitable.
10. Avoid distractions.
11. Don't get demoralized.
12. Don't give up.
13. Deals fall through.
